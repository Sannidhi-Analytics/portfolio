Quality Assurance Case Studies

Three worked examples from GMP practice, written up in situation–action–result form. Details are generalised to protect employer confidentiality; the approach and outcomes are my own.

Reducing deviation resolution time by 30%

Quality Associate · Pharmaceutical manufacturing · GMP-regulated site

Situation

Deviation investigations were closing slowly and inconsistently. Some were resolved in days, others drifted for weeks past target. The pattern behind the delay was that investigations frequently stalled at the point of establishing root cause: an investigation would be opened, initial information gathered, and then it would sit while the investigator waited on input from Manufacturing or Engineering that had never been formally requested. A secondary problem was that a proportion of investigations closed on "operator error" as the stated cause, which is rarely a true root cause and almost never supports a meaningful corrective action.

Action

I approached this as a process problem rather than an effort problem. I reviewed the open and recently closed deviation population to identify where time was actually being lost, and found the delay concentrated in the investigation phase rather than in approval or documentation. I introduced a more structured investigation approach: identifying at the point of opening which functions needed to contribute, requesting that input explicitly with a date rather than informally, and applying structured root cause questioning so that investigations pushed past the first plausible explanation to the underlying system failure. Where "operator error" was proposed, I asked what in the procedure, the training, or the working environment had made the error possible. I also coordinated directly across Manufacturing, QC and Supply Chain to unblock cases waiting on information, and tracked open deviations against monthly closure targets so that ageing cases became visible to management rather than quietly drifting.

Result

Average deviation resolution time fell by 30%. Investigation quality improved alongside speed, which matters more than the headline number — corrective actions addressed system causes such as procedural ambiguity and documentation gaps rather than restating that someone made a mistake. The structured questioning approach was picked up informally by operational colleagues, which extended the benefit beyond the cases I handled personally.

Achieving zero major findings across internal audits

Quality Associate · Change control and audit readiness

Situation

I managed change control end-to-end for process and system modifications, across a documentation estate of more than 80 GMP-regulated documents spanning four departments. Change control is where audit findings are typically generated, because it is the point at which a documented system either keeps pace with what the site actually does or falls behind it. The risk is not usually a badly assessed change — it is a change implemented correctly on the floor while the associated documents, training records, or validation status quietly fall out of alignment.

Action

I treated audit readiness as a continuous state rather than a pre-audit exercise. For each change I reviewed inputs against release criteria and applicable regulatory standards, and traced the full downstream impact: which SOPs and work instructions required revision, which personnel needed retraining, and what evidence would demonstrate the change was implemented as approved. I closed each change only when that evidence existed, rather than when the physical change was complete. Alongside this, I reviewed documentation for recurring gaps and found that certain work instructions were ambiguous enough to be interpreted differently by different shifts — a latent finding waiting to be discovered. I supported rewriting those instructions to align with FDA and EMA expectations. During audits themselves I represented the QA function and explained the rationale behind our compliance practices to auditors directly.

Result

Zero major findings across all internal audits during my time in the role, with a 98% audit-ready documentation rate maintained across the estate. Because readiness was continuous, audit preparation did not require the disruptive scramble that pulls a quality team away from routine work for weeks beforehand.

Holding compliance without stopping production

Quality Associate · Cross-functional working, 25+ quality cases per week

Situation

The tension every QA function lives with is that quality decisions have production consequences. Processing 25 or more quality cases a week across Manufacturing, QC and Supply Chain meant regular situations where a batch was waiting, a shift was under schedule pressure, and the quality answer was not immediately obvious. Handled badly, this produces one of two failure modes: QA becomes an obstacle that operations routes around, or QA becomes accommodating and compliance erodes at the margins.

Action

My working approach was to be fast on the things that could be fast and immovable on the things that could not. Straightforward queries I turned around quickly, because a QA function with a reputation for slow responses invites people to stop asking. Where a genuine compliance question arose, I was explicit about what the standard required and why, and focused on finding a compliant route forward rather than simply refusing. That usually meant getting the right people into the conversation early — Engineering for equipment questions, QC for analytical ones — instead of passing documents back and forth. I also coached operational colleagues informally on GMP requirements, on the reasoning that a floor which understands why a requirement exists generates fewer deviations than one that has merely been told to follow it.

Result

100% GMP compliance maintained across the case volume, with quality issues resolved without impacting production schedules. The more durable outcome was relational: Manufacturing and Supply Chain colleagues raised potential issues with me early rather than after the fact, which is the point at which a quality function can actually prevent problems instead of documenting them.
