Findings

What three years of enforcement reveals about regulatory priorities.

Financial crime systems and controls failures attract the largest penalties — £151.6m across ten actions

Anti-money-laundering and financial crime control failings account for 43% of all fine value from just 17% of actions. Nationwide (£44.1m), Barclays Bank plc (£39.3m), Starling (£29.0m) and Metro Bank (£16.7m) dominate. The pattern is consistent: the FCA reserves its heaviest penalties for firms whose control frameworks failed to detect or prevent criminal misuse.

Firms pay 21 times more than individuals, but individuals are pursued far more often than fine value suggests

Firms absorbed £337.8m against £15.8m for individuals — yet individuals account for 29 of the 60 actions, almost exactly half. Enforcement against people is frequent but individually modest, and it is nearly always paired with a prohibition order, which is the real sanction. A £6,000 fine that ends a career is not a light penalty.

Unsuitable pension transfer advice is the most common breach type by count — 12 of 60 actions

Pensions advice failures generated more separate enforcement actions than any other category, but only £11.7m in total value, because they overwhelmingly target individual advisers rather than institutions. This is the long tail of the defined benefit transfer scandal working through the enforcement pipeline.

Enforcement value is volatile year to year — £53.4m, then £176.0m, then £124.2m

2024 saw more than triple 2023's total. Single large penalties move the annual figure substantially, so year-on-year comparison of headline totals says more about which investigations concluded that year than about any shift in regulatory appetite. Action counts (12, 27, 21) are a steadier signal than fine value.

Retail and investment banking carry the heaviest penalty burden

Retail banking (£135.1m) and investment banking (£121.6m) together account for 72.6% of all fine value. Both sectors combine large customer bases, complex control environments, and high financial crime exposure — the conditions under which control failures become expensive.

Principle 3 — adequate systems and controls — is the FCA's most-used enforcement tool

Reading across the Final Notices, breaches of Principle 3 (or the associated SYSC rules requiring firms to organise and control their affairs responsibly with adequate risk management systems) underpin the majority of the largest penalties. Firms are rarely fined for a single bad outcome; they are fined because the system that should have caught it did not exist or did not work.

Why a pharma QA professional built this

Regulated is regulated. The FCA fining a bank for inadequate systems and controls under Principle 3 is doing the same thing the MHRA or FDA does when it cites a manufacturer for CGMP deviations: identifying that a control framework failed to do what it was designed to do. The vocabulary differs — Final Notice instead of Form 483, SYSC instead of Annex 1 — but the underlying discipline is identical. Define the control, evidence that it operates, investigate honestly when it fails, and fix the cause rather than the symptom.

This analysis exists to demonstrate that the transfer is real and that I have done the reading. The categorisation required understanding what PRIN 2 and PRIN 3 actually require, why a transaction reporting breach under MiFIR sits in a different risk category to a market abuse case, and why the FCA pairs individual fines with prohibitions.

Method & data provenance Real regulatory data

Every record was transcribed from the FCA's published annual fines pages for 2023, 2024 and 2025. Each entry lists the entity fined, date, penalty amount, and a summary of the breach.

Validation: computed annual totals reconcile exactly to the FCA's own published figures — £53,354,600 for 2023, £176,045,385 for 2024, and £124,221,367 for 2025. This confirms the dataset is complete and correctly transcribed rather than a partial sample.

Breach themes and sectors were assigned by reading each Final Notice summary and grouping by the substantive regulatory failure. This is judgement-based categorisation, not automated, and another analyst might reasonably group a handful of borderline cases differently. Court fines (two small data protection prosecutions in 2025) are excluded, consistent with the FCA's own treatment, as they were imposed by a court rather than issued by the FCA.
